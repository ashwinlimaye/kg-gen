Core Entities 	 

Market Space 

Market Space represents the “where to play” scope of the business – a multidimensional definition of the arena in which the company competes. It is defined by a specific combination of customer segments, geographic regions, distribution channels, product/service categories, and customer needs​ 

In other words, a market space is a distinct subset of the market that the firm targets (e.g. a certain customer demographic in a region via online channels with particular product types). This entity is core to the ontology as it captures the breadth of the current playing field. It is designed to be flexible and hierarchical, so that broad market spaces can be broken into narrower sub-spaces (by segment, region, etc.) as needed. This scalability ensures the ontology can adapt to different contexts – from a niche local market to a global multi-segment arena. 

Market Space Attributes (Dynamic Size & Attractiveness) 

Each Market Space entity (a distinct industry segment or “where we play”) is characterized by two key attributes: Size and Attractiveness. These are modelled to capture historical data and future projections, and to reflect competitive conditions: 

Size (Time-Series Data): Represent market size as a time-series property with past, current, and future values. For example, the ontology can link a Market Space to yearly size data (e.g. revenue or volume by year), enabling trend analysis over time. This structure supports capturing growth or decline trajectories and can be updated as new data becomes available. The time-series approach ensures scalability in temporal data and allows analyzing trends over time (e.g. steady growth, cyclical patterns). 

Size (Trend-Based Projection): In addition to raw data points, model future projected size as an attribute influenced by external trends. Each Market Space can have a forecasted size (or growth rate) that is explicitly linked to External Trend factors driving expansion or contraction. For instance, a projection might note that “Market Space Y is expected to grow +5% annually, driven by Trend X (e.g. demographic shifts or technological adoption).” This link to trend drivers makes the projection transparent and explainable – changes in the macro environment or technology can be traced to their impact on market size. It effectively embeds PESTEL factors into market forecasts, so the ontology can adapt as external forces change. 

Attractiveness: Model market attractiveness as an indicator of the segment’s average profitability potential. In strategy terms, attractiveness reflects the overall industry profit opportunity available in that market​ 

It can be represented as a rating or score (quantitative or qualitative) for each Market Space. This attribute is influenced by competitive forces – e.g. if Porter’s Five Forces analysis indicates intense rivalry, high buyer/supplier power, or many substitutes, the attractiveness (profitability) will be lower​ 

Conversely, markets with favorable forces (e.g. weak competition or high barriers to entry) get a higher attractiveness score. The ontology can either store a composite attractiveness value or break it down by factors (to remain flexible across industries). Because attractiveness is an aggregate measure of industry profitability, it remains compatible with standard frameworks – users can adjust it based on five-forces evaluations or sector benchmarks. 

Distinctive Choices 

Distinctive Choices are the explicit or implicit strategic decisions that define the breadth vs. focus of the playing field. They capture where the firm chooses to compete or not compete, shaping the unique scope of its strategy. Strategy literature emphasizes that no company can succeed by trying to be “all things to all people,” underscoring the need to narrow the competitive field and focus on specific markets​ 

These choices may involve, for example, focusing on premium segments and ignoring low-end segments or specializing in certain product categories. Such decisions differentiate the firm’s approach from competitors by clarifying which market spaces it will prioritize. Distinctive choices can often be observed in what a company deliberately forgoes. For instance, Procter & Gamble’s Bounty brand chose not to pursue the lowest-price customer segment to avoid diluting its premium brand, a strategic choice that allowed focus on more lucrative segments​ 

By modelling these choices, the ontology can represent the company’s strategic boundaries – the edges of its current playing field – and how broad or narrow its engagement is across the available market spaces. 

Opportunities and Threats 

Opportunities and Threats in the ontology are external elements linked to the playing field, contextualizing the market spaces against the broader environment. Each opportunity or threat is associated with one or more specific market space segments (a subset of the playing field) and with relevant external factors or trends that give rise to it. For example, a new technology trend might be modelled as an opportunity linked to the product category it could impact, while a regulatory change could appear as a threat tied to the geography it affects. We draw on established analysis frameworks to identify these factors: a PESTEL analysis (Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental, Legal) is one common tool for organizing macro-environmental trends and understanding how they influence industries and firms​ 

Similarly, Porter’s Five Forces framework highlights industry forces (e.g. threat of new entrants or substitutes, buyer/supplier power, competitive rivalry) which often manifest as opportunities or threats in a given market space​ 

In the ontology, each Opportunity/Threat entity is linked both to its source in the external environment (e.g. a specific trend or force) and to the market space subset it impacts. Importantly, the model recognizes that whether an external factor is seen as an opportunity or a threat can be subjective, depending on the firm’s perspective and strategy. Executives may interpret the same trend differently: for one company an emerging technology is an opportunity to expand, while for another it’s a threat to the status quo. The ontology allows tagging these entities with qualitative attributes or commentary (capturing the executive perspective on the factor), ensuring that the subjective interpretation is preserved. This design provides flexibility for different business contexts – as external conditions change or leaders reassess them, new opportunities/threats can be added or reclassified without altering the core structure. 

 

 

Competitors 

Competitors are represented in two ways: as individual competitor entities (specific named companies) and as competition clusters that group similar competitors. Individual competitor entities capture direct rivals (e.g. Company A, Company B), with attributes like their name, profile, and perhaps key strengths/weaknesses. These competitors are linked to the market spaces where they operate – indicating which segments or regions each competitor is playing in – and to any distinctive choices or strategies they are known for (e.g. a competitor might focus on a niche, or have made a choice to dominate a particular channel). In addition, the ontology includes Competitor Cluster entities to model groups of competitors that share similar strategic characteristics. In strategy analysis, firms often cluster into strategic groups – companies within an industry that have comparable strategies, market positions, and characteristics​ 

For example, in the consulting industry a “Big 4” cluster can represent the large, broad-service firms, while a “Boutique” cluster represents smaller firms focused on specialized services. These cluster entities allow the ontology to capture competition at a higher level of abstraction (e.g. comparing how broad-market incumbents vs. niche players each pose different threats in various market spaces). Each competitor cluster is linked to the member companies (individual competitor entities) and can also be connected to market spaces (if the cluster as a whole tends to operate in certain segments). This dual modelling of competition provides scalability: one can analyze specific competitor dynamics or look at the aggregate competitive forces exerted by a group of similar players. 

Example: A strategic group map for the restaurant industry, illustrating competitor clusters. Each oval represents a cluster of companies with similar strategies – for instance, the red oval (top right) groups fast-food chains competing on low price and quick service, whereas the orange oval (bottom left) groups mid-scale restaurants with higher prices and broader menus. In our ontology, such clusters can be modeled as competitor group entities (with members like McDonald’s or Wendy’s in one cluster vs. Olive Garden or Red Lobster in another). This approach enables flexible analysis of competition, whether focusing on individual rivals or the characteristics of a whole group. 

Value Systems 
A Value System represents the broader industry ecosystem in which the company operates. It is defined as an interconnected network of entities – including suppliers, partners, competitors, distributors, and customers – that collectively produce and deliver value within a shared industry context​ 

This concept (akin to an industry-level value chain) is not a standalone node but a web of relationships: each company’s value chain is embedded in a larger stream of activities or “value system” encompassing upstream suppliers and downstream channels to buyers​ 

Key attributes of the Value System include: 

Industry Classification – A label or code identifying the industry (e.g. Pharmaceuticals, Automotive, Enterprise Software). This situates the value system in a known domain and guides which market rules or norms apply. For example, a Value System classified as Pharmaceuticals would imply a context of drug R&D, manufacturing, and distribution, distinct from Automotive. Industry classification provides context for applicable trends and benchmarks, ensuring each Value System is analyzed with the right frame of reference. 

Supplier & Buyer Structure – A description of the upstream supply base and downstream customer base characterizing that industry. This captures how value flows and who holds power in the network. For instance, does the industry rely on a few critical suppliers or many fragmented ones? Are the buyers concentrated (few large customers) or dispersed mass-market consumers? These structural features influence competitive dynamics (e.g. highly concentrated suppliers can exert strong bargaining power over industry firms​ 

By modeling supplier/buyer structure, the ontology can represent factors like supplier power and buyer power directly, rather than treating them only abstractly. This attribute aligns with analysis frameworks (such as Porter’s Five Forces) that consider supplier and buyer power as key to industry profitability​ 

Regulatory Constraints – Any overarching regulations, standards, or legal factors that apply to the entire value system. Many industries have shared constraints (for example, pharmaceuticals must comply with drug safety regulations, finance with banking laws). If applicable, the Value System entity would note the presence of strict regulatory oversight or industry-specific compliance requirements. This attribute ensures that the ontology captures external legal/political factors (the “L” in PESTEL) as inherent context for the industry​ 

It flags that companies operating in this value system face these non-negotiable constraints, which can shape strategy (e.g. barriers to entry, cost of compliance) and influence what is considered an opportunity or threat. 

Scope of Offerings – The major Product/Service categories or segments encompassed by this Value System. Each Value System can be associated with the types of products or services typical for that industry, mapping the company’s offerings to the industry structure. For example, an Automotive Value System might include vehicle categories and auto parts, whereas an Enterprise Software Value System covers software product categories and related services. This attribute (or linked list) ensures that all Product/Service Categories in the ontology are tied to a relevant industry context. In other words, any product or service category the company deals with should align with one or more Value Systems, making clear how each offering fits within the industry’s value network. By defining the offerings scope, we embed the company’s portfolio within the proper industry ecosystem for strategic analysis. 

(By treating the Value System as a first-class entity with these attributes, the ontology can explicitly represent the industry environment as a network of players and constraints, rather than implicitly assuming a single industry context. It provides a container for industry-wide properties like typical value chain structure and rules of the game.) 

 

Relationships Between Entities 

The ontology defines clear relationships to interconnect the above core entities, reflecting how elements of the playing field influence each other: 

Market Space ↔ External Factors/Forces: Each market space is linked to relevant external trends and competitive forces that shape its dynamics. For example, a particular market space (say, youth consumers in Europe for mobile services) might be linked to technological trends (e.g. 5G adoption rates), economic factors (consumer spending levels), and industry forces (e.g. high competitive rivalry or threat of new entrants in that domain). These links ensure that for any given section of the playing field, one can trace which external opportunities or threats (trends, regulatory changes, new technologies, etc.) are in play. This mirrors the use of tools like Porter’s Five Forces within the model – e.g. a high Threat of Substitutes or Bargaining Power of Buyers would be captured as linked threat entities for the affected market space. By structuring these relationships, the ontology stays compatible with industry analysis frameworks and can be easily updated as external conditions evolve (maintaining flexibility across different business environments). 

Competitors ↔ Market Spaces and Choices: Competitors (individuals and clusters) are mapped to the market spaces where they compete, indicating which segments or regions each competitor targets. This relationship establishes the competitive landscape per segment: for any given market space, one can retrieve the list of competitors present. Furthermore, competitors are linked to the distinctive choices they have made (where known). For instance, a competitor cluster might be associated with a choice to offer a broad product line versus a focused specialization, or an individual competitor might be noted as pursuing a specific channel or niche as their strategy. These links allow analysis of how a company’s own playing field choices compare to competitors’ choices. If a firm’s distinctive choice is to focus on high-end customers in a niche geography, the ontology can highlight which competitors have made similar or contrasting choices (perhaps a cluster of low-cost broad players covers the whole market, posing a different competitive challenge). This relationship design helps in exploring questions like: Are there market spaces with heavy competitor presence (crowded field) versus open spaces? or How do competitor strategic positions align or conflict with our own choices? By representing competitor ties in this way, the ontology remains scalable – new competitors or new strategic moves can be added and linked without restructuring, and it’s adaptable to any industry (the competition links can accommodate direct rivals, substitute providers, new entrants, etc., as needed). 

Opportunities/Threats ↔ Market Spaces (Contextualized by Perspective): Each opportunity or threat is mapped to the specific market space(s) it affects. This relationship grounds abstract external factors in the context of the business’s current playing field. For example, a new regulatory policy might be linked specifically to the geography and product category it impacts (a threat to the European market for a certain product line), or a trend like rising health consciousness could be an opportunity linked to the customer segment it makes more receptive to the company’s offerings. Additionally, the ontology can link an opportunity/threat to the executive or stakeholder perspective that characterized it as such – effectively capturing the rationale. While this latter relationship may be implemented as an annotation, it ensures that the subjectivity (why something is seen as an opportunity or threat) is not lost. The same external factor might be linked to multiple market spaces and flagged as an opportunity in one context but a threat in another. By mapping opportunities and threats in a fine-grained way, the ontology supports scenario analysis and adaptability: as new external factors emerge or as the business enters new market spaces, new opportunity/threat entities can be introduced and connected accordingly. This keeps the strategy model aligned with the changing environment, maintaining both flexibility (easy to extend with new factors) and clarity (each factor is contextualized to relevant parts of the playing field). 

Opportunities & Threats (Trend Impacts & Quantification) 

explicit cause-effect links between External Trends and market attributes, and it quantifies Opportunities and Threats for the company in each market space. The goal is to make the ontology analytical – connecting how external changes translate into gains or risks: 

External Trend Entities: Model major external factors (PESTEL categories: Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental, Legal) as first-class entities in the graph​ 

Each External Trend node represents a specific context trend or force (e.g. “Electric Vehicle Adoption”, “New Emissions Regulation”, “Changing Consumer Preference for SUVs”). These trend nodes can have their own attributes (e.g. description, trend trajectory, category type) and timeline (if the trend is expected to occur within a certain period). 

Causal Relationships – Trends → Market Attributes: Introduce a relationship (e.g. influences or impacts) from an External Trend to Market Space attributes. This allows encoding context-specific causality: Trend X influences the future Size (or Attractiveness) of Market Space Y. For example, if Trend = “Electric Vehicle (EV) Adoption”, it might be linked to Market Space = “Automotive Catalytic Converters” with an “influences (negative)” relationship on market Size. This explicitly captures that the EV adoption trend is expected to reduce the future size of the catalytic converter market. Each such relationship can carry qualifiers like impact type (e.g. positive/negative) or magnitude. This granular linkage makes the ontology explainable – analysts can trace an opportunity or threat back to the specific external cause. It also aligns with PESTEL thinking by showing how macro forces shape industry dynamics (e.g. a new technology trend affecting industry growth). 

Opportunity Quantification: For every Market Space, define the Opportunity for the company by linking the company’s current position to future market potential. In the ontology, the company’s involvement in a Market Space is captured by a relationship (e.g. Company ➝ participates_in ➝ MarketSpace) with attributes for current revenue and profit contributed by that segment. The Opportunity can then be quantified as a function of projected market size and attractiveness. In practice, one can introduce a property (or calculated node) like opportunityPotential on that relationship. This value could represent the future revenue/profit upside for the company if the market grows as projected and remains attractive. It essentially answers: “How much could we gain if we ride this trend?” For example, if the company currently earns $50M in Market Y and Market Y is projected (due to favorable trends) to double in size in five years, the opportunity might be roughly an additional $50M (assuming the company sustains share). The ontology thus supports scenario analysis – by adjusting the External Trend assumptions, the opportunityPotential would update accordingly. 

Threat Quantification: Similarly, model Threats as the downside risk in each Market Space. This can be represented with a threatExposure attribute tied to the company’s participation in a market, quantifying potential revenue or profit loss. Threats are derived from trends or competitive actions that shrink the market or erode the company’s position. For instance, if external analysis shows the market’s size or profitability will decline (negative attractiveness shift), the company’s current $X in that space is at risk. A trend like “Shift to electric vehicles” implies a major threat to any business reliant on internal combustion engine components: one could estimate “by 2030 this market may drop by 80%, so $X of our current revenue is endangered.”​ 

Disruptive trends (like substitutes or new technology) can drastically reduce a market’s profitability​ and the ontology captures this by linking those trends to a negative size/attractiveness change, which in turn feeds into the threatExposure value. Additionally, company-specific risks can be modeled – e.g. a relationship indicating a Competitor’s new technology threatens a particular product line. This can be done by treating a competitor innovation as a specialized External Trend (Technological) that negatively influences the market or directly the company’s offering. The Threat quantification would then include lost business from that competitive disruption (for example, if a competitor’s alternative steals 20% market share, the revenue corresponding to that 20% is the threat to the company). By quantifying threats in this way, the ontology enables clear identification of high-risk areas, aligning with SWOT analysis (the “Threats” quadrant) but in a data-driven manner. 

Opportunity/Threat as Nodes (Optional): Depending on implementation, Opportunities and Threats can also be instantiated as separate entities linked to the company. For example, an “Opportunity” node could connect Company -> MarketSpace -> Trend to explicitly model the scenario “Trend X creates Opportunity Y in Market Z”. Similarly, a “Threat” node could link a specific trend or competitor action to a loss scenario for the company. This approach can capture rich context (like tagging an opportunity with which trend enables it, or a threat with which competitor causes it). However, even without separate nodes, the combination of market attributes and the above relationships is sufficient to derive quantified opportunity/threat metrics. 

Competitor Modeling (Participation & Roles) 

To robustly capture the competitive landscape, the ontology’s competitor modeling is enhanced to allow both binary presence in markets and qualitative roles of competitors: 

Competitor Entities: Model Competitor as an entity (likely of the same class as “Company” but distinguished as rivals). Each Competitor node would have basic attributes (name, etc.) and possibly metadata like type of competitor (e.g. incumbent firm, startup, etc.) if needed. Competitors are linked to Market Spaces to denote where they operate. 

Market Participation (Binary): Introduce a relationship such as Competitor ➝ plays_in ➝ MarketSpace (or active_in) to indicate that a given competitor participates in a specific market space. If a competitor is not present in that market, simply no such relationship exists – this effectively provides a binary model of participation (presence vs. absence). This yes/no modeling is useful for mapping the competitive field at a high level (who are the players in Market X?). It keeps the ontology scalable because you can add many competitors across many markets without complexity – each link is a binary flag of presence. 

Role Qualifiers (Incumbent, New Entrant, etc.): To add nuance, the participation relationship can carry a qualifier attribute (or subtype) describing the competitor’s role or intensity in that market. For example, a property participationType on the relationship could be an enumeration: “Incumbent” (established major player), “Dominant” (market leader), “Marginal Player” (minor market share), “New Entrant” (recently entered or exploring). This allows the ontology to distinguish, say, a long-time dominant competitor from a small niche player, rather than treating both as equal participants. These qualifiers support deeper analysis – e.g. incumbents might influence the market’s attractiveness differently than new entrants (new entrants increase competitive intensity, aligning with Porter’s “threat of new entrants”). If needed, additional attributes like market share or competitive advantage can further qualify the competitor’s position, but the ontology keeps them optional to remain adaptable. The presence of multiple competitors and their roles can feed into the Attractiveness attribute of the market (more competitors or aggressive new entrants typically lower average profitability). 

Company’s Competitors: The ontology can also relate competitors to the focal Company (for instance, a relationship Competitor ➝ competes_with ➝ Company in Market X). However, since Current Playing Field focuses on the external view, it’s often sufficient to link competitors to markets and infer competitive relationships via common market participation. If the company and a competitor both link to the same MarketSpace, they are competitors in that space. The binary + qualifier approach ensures the model remains simple but extensible – one can quickly toggle a competitor’s presence on or off in analysis, and add detail if a more granular competitive assessment is needed. 

Competitor entities are a type of Company, which are linked to Value Systems with a role of “competitor.” Moreover, we should account for other company roles: for example, introduce that some companies in the value system might be our partners or suppliers rather than direct rivals, and these too can be represented in the ontology. If the ontology document has a diagram or discussion of competitor links, we can enhance it by showing supplier and partner links as well. The goal is to evolve competitor modeling into a more comprehensive Industry Player modeling, where competitors are one category of players in the value system. This will enrich analyses of competition by including potential collaborators and the supply chain context. Questions the document poses (like “How crowded is this field?” or “Who else influences this space?”) can now be answered not just by listing competitors, but also by considering if key suppliers or dominant buyers are shaping the playing field. 

Companies ↔ Value System (Role Association): Each Company in the ontology (whether the focal firm, a competitor, a supplier, or a partner) should be linked to one or more Value Systems representing the industries it participates in. This relationship carries a role attribute to specify the company’s function in that industry – for example, Supplier, Buyer (Customer), Competitor, Partner, etc. A single company can have multiple roles across different value systems (or even within one value system, a company might be both a supplier and a competitor in different contexts). For instance, a tech giant like Microsoft might be linked to the Enterprise Software Value System as a Competitor (to our firm’s software products) and simultaneously linked to a PC Manufacturing Value System as a Supplier (if providing operating systems to PC makers). By modeling these roles explicitly, the ontology captures the full ecosystem relationships – not only direct competitors but also who supplies whom and who partners with whom in delivering end value. This enriches competitor modeling into a broader industry player modeling, recognizing that today’s supplier could be tomorrow’s rival, and partners in one market may compete in another. It also aligns with the idea of the value system as a network: each company node connects into the industry web with a specific role, rather than floating independently. 

Product/Service Category ↔ Value System: All Product or Service Category entities should be associated with the Value System that represents their industry. This mapping ensures that each category of offering is analyzed in the correct industry context. For example, if the company has a “Cloud Database Software” product category, it would be linked to an Enterprise Software Value System, whereas a “Pharmaceuticals” product category links to the Pharmaceutical Value System. This relationship essentially tags every offering with the industry ecosystem it belongs to. It provides traceability from a product category to relevant industry attributes like typical supplier/buyer structure and regulatory environment. It also helps identify the industry-specific competitors and market spaces for that product. In the ontology, this could be implemented by linking the Product/Service Category entity to the Value System entity (e.g. Category X is part of ValueSystem Y). The result is a more contextualized view of the company’s portfolio: one can query an industry (Value System) and see all related product categories (and thus which parts of the business fall under that industry’s domain). This alignment of offerings to Value Systems reinforces consistent strategic reasoning – ensuring, for instance, that when analyzing trends or forces, you consider the correct industry context for each offering. 

Market Space ↔ Value System: Market Spaces (the segments/regions where the firm plays) should be tied to the corresponding Value System to bridge segment-level analysis with industry-level context. In practice, a Market Space is often a subset of an industry (Value System) defined by specific customer segment and region. By linking a Market Space to a Value System, we clarify which industry’s value network that market space is a part of. For example, a market space defined as “Electric SUVs in Europe” would link to the Automotive Value System, indicating that this segment is within the automotive industry’s ecosystem. This connection is useful because it means external factors or competitive moves that are defined at the industry level (value system) can be inherited or connected to the relevant market spaces. It also helps cluster related market spaces under their broader industry for navigation in the ontology. Essentially, each Market Space is contextualized by an industry Value System, complementing the multi-dimensional market definition with an industry dimension. 

Value System ↔ Value System (Interdependencies): Many industries are interlinked, supplying and depending on each other, and the ontology should reflect these dependencies between Value Systems. We can model this with relationships between Value System entities, such as ValueSystem A depends on ValueSystem B (or supplies / feeds). For instance, the Pharmaceutical Value System depends on the Chemical Manufacturing Value System for raw materials (specialty chemicals and active ingredients). Similarly, an Automotive Value System today depends on the Semiconductor/Electronics Value System for key components (microchips). By adding these links, the ontology captures upstream/downstream industry relationships. This is crucial for strategic insight: a disruption in one value system can pose a threat to another (e.g. semiconductor shortages threatening automotive production), or a boom in one can create an opportunity in another (e.g. growth in electric vehicle value system boosting the battery manufacturing value system). These inter-value-system links enable modeling of cross-industry Opportunities and Threats and help identify where value is added across industries. In graph terms, it creates a higher-level network of industries, showing how the company’s playing field (and its risks/opportunities) might be influenced by adjacent industries in the broader economy. 

External Factors ↔ Value System: (This extends how we link trends or forces in the ontology.) External trends, regulations, or technological changes often affect entire industries (value systems). With the Value System entity in place, we can link relevant External Trend or Factor nodes directly to the Value System to denote industry-wide impact. For example, a new data privacy law could be linked to the Social Media Value System (if it affects all social media companies), or a trend of “health consciousness” could link to the Food & Beverage Value System. While previously the ontology linked external factors to Market Spaces or directly to opportunities/threats, linking them to Value Systems adds another layer: it flags that a trend is broadly influencing the industry’s ecosystem. Those influences can then propagate to specific market segments or companies through the connections we’ve established (for instance, if a Value System has multiple Market Spaces, each can inherit the trend impact). This relationship ensures the ontology captures industry-level external forces in a structured way, complementing the fine-grained Market Space links. It reinforces that the Value System is the scope at which many external forces (like regulatory shifts or supplier market changes) operate. 

Opportunities & Threats ↔ Value System.   Opportunities or threats might arise from changes in the Value System structure itself – for example, a consolidation of suppliers (an upstream value system change) could pose a threat by increasing their power, or a new distribution partnership (within the value system) could be an opportunity. Additionally, Regulatory Constraints captured as a value system attribute can be linked to threat entities (e.g. “new regulation = threat” for all companies in that value system). By integrating value systems here, we ensure that macro changes like regulatory shifts or supplier market disruptions are explicitly considered in the opportunity/threat model, not just high-level trends. For instance, if the ontology previously noted “bargaining power of suppliers” as a factor lowering market attractiveness​, we can now model this concretely: a high supplier power can be represented by the value system relationship (few suppliers) and any worsening of that (say a key supplier raising prices) can be logged as a Threat event linked to the Value System. In summary, incorporating value system links makes the opportunities/threats more traceable – one can point to which part of the value chain is causing the issue or opportunity. This also ties into scenario analysis: if a dependent value system suffers a disruption, our ontology can surface the downstream threat to the focal value system’s market spaces. 

By establishing these relationships, the Value System becomes a central integrative concept. Companies are no longer just competitors in a vacuum; they are participants in value systems with defined roles. Products are grounded in the industry context of their value system. And value systems themselves connect, reflecting real-world supply chains and industry linkages. The ontology therefore moves toward a network-of-industries view, where one can traverse from our company to its suppliers, to those suppliers’ suppliers, and so on, mapping a full ecosystem graph​ 

This network structure is richer than a flat list of competitors or market spaces – it captures how entities interact to create value, which is vital for strategy formulation. 

 

Framework Alignment and Scalability 

Finally, the refined ontology is designed to be compatible with major strategy frameworks and adaptable across industries: 

PESTEL Integration: External Trend nodes directly map to PESTEL factors (Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental, Legal)​ 

 This means any macro-environment factor identified in a PESTEL analysis can be added to the graph as a node and linked to the relevant markets. The ontology thus serves as a living PESTEL map – if a new regulation or technology emerges, you introduce it as a Trend entity and connect it to all affected Market Spaces. This keeps the strategy knowledge graph up-to-date with the external environment and ensures no major trend is overlooked (addressing the need to “monitor macro-environmental factors that impact the organization”). 

Porter’s Five Forces Alignment: The Attractiveness attribute of Market Spaces is essentially a reflection of industry profitability as determined by the Five Forces​ 

By modeling competitor presence (rivalry), new entrants, threat of substitutes (captured via trend influences, e.g. technological substitution)​, and even supplier/buyer power (these could be added as needed via additional nodes or attributes), the ontology mirrors a Five Forces analysis within its structure. For instance, a high number of competitors (many plays_in links) with strong roles would imply high rivalry, potentially lowering the attractiveness score. A trend representing a substitute product can be linked as a threat (similar to “threat of substitutes” in Five Forces)​ 

This compatibility means strategy practitioners can use the ontology to systematically evaluate each of Porter’s forces and see their impact on market profitability and company strategy. 

Scalability: The ontology uses generic entity types (Market Space, Company, Competitor, External Trend) that can be instantiated for any industry or market segment. New industries would simply mean new MarketSpace nodes and possibly new trend factors, without changing the schema. The time-series structure for market size can accommodate any number of time points (past or future), making it suitable for long-range planning or fast-moving markets alike. Because relationships (like influences or participates_in) are many-to-many, the model naturally scales to complex scenarios: a single trend can impact multiple markets, and a single market can be influenced by multiple trends – all are just additional edges in the graph. Similarly, many competitors can be linked to many markets without altering the core ontology design. 

Adaptability: The ontology is extensible – additional attributes or relationship types can be added as needed for specific contexts. For example, if a certain industry needs to track regulatory licenses or specific risk factors, one can introduce those as extra nodes or properties (the base structure won’t break). The design separates concerns (markets, external factors, players), so updating one aspect (say adding a new trend or competitor) doesn’t require reworking others. This modularity aligns well with strategic analysis tools – one can plug in a new analysis dimension (like a COVID-19 pandemic node as an External Trend affecting multiple markets) seamlessly. 

Readable and Explainable: By structuring data in this ontology, the Current Playing Field becomes a knowledge graph that is both machine-readable and human-intelligible. An executive could query, for example, “Which market spaces are growing due to technological trends, and what is our exposure in each?” and the graph would highlight the Market Spaces with positive influenced_by links from Tech trends and show the company’s revenues (opportunities) there. The explicit links and attributes ensure that any strategic insight (e.g. “Market A is attractive and growing, so it’s an opportunity”) is backed by data nodes and relationships that explain why (e.g. “because Trend X is driving growth and there are few competitors, leading to high profitability”). This transparency and structured approach are consistent with best-practice frameworks, but more importantly, they make the strategy actionable and verifiable. 

In summary, the Current Playing Field ontology comprises entities for Market Spaces, External Trends, the Company and Competitors and Value Systems; attributes like Size (with time-series values and trend-based projections) and Attractiveness on markets, and performance metrics on the company’s market links; and relationships that map out who plays in each market and how external forces cause market changes. This structure quantitatively captures Opportunities and Threats as emergent properties of the graph. It is a scalable, flexible foundation that can evolve with different industries and remain aligned with established strategy analysis frameworks – enabling both AI systems and human strategists to reason about the company’s current playing field in a rigorous, data-driven way.
